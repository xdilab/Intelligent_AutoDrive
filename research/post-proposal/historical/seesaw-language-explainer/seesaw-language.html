Who sets the balance?

Stage 5 (Visual Composition Expert) and Stage 4 (Phrase Expert) see the same crop feature, but can give different answers. The blend decides how much each answer contributes. The surprising part is that the rule for choosing that balance can matter as much as the experts themselves.

1

The big picture: combine two useful opinions.

Think of each end as a source of influence. More weight on one side means more of that expert’s prediction enters the final score.

Left end · frozen expert

Stage 5: Visual Composition Expert

Flat head + Composition MLP. The MLP receives the crop feature and 49 flat primitive scores, then predicts 135 composition scores.

No explicit phrase-score branch. Its encoder does have vision–language pretraining, so “completely language-free” would be inaccurate.

Right end · frozen expert

Stage 4: Phrase Expert

Phrase head on crop features. A learned projection compares the crop feature with fixed class-phrase embeddings to produce label scores. No Composition MLP.

It outputs class scores. It is not writing a caption or reasoning aloud during this experiment.

The blend is a new component on top of Stage 5. We call it the Gated Composition Head, or GCH. Stage 6: Phrase-Fusion MLP is the alternative baseline. Its MLP receives crop features, flat primitive scores, and phrase composition scores. The seesaw instead blends the finished Stage 5 and Stage 4 composition predictions.
2

You control the seesaw.

Move the balance toward either expert. The lower end represents more influence. This is a teaching metaphor, not a physical model of training.

0.50 × Stage 5 + 0.50 × Stage 4 = blended prediction

This percentage is an expert’s contribution, not confidence that the expert is correct. A global blend uses this same balance for every crop and all 135 composition classes. A class-wise gate uses one balance per class. Neither changes its balance in response to the scene.

3

The judges want different things.

BCE asks whether the probability values are accurate. AP asks whether the real positives rise above the negatives in the ranking.

Judge A · BCE loss

“How much probability did you put on every yes and no?”

A missing label with a high score gets penalized. A present label with a low score gets penalized. In ordinary mean BCE, every crop/class pair contributes equally.

Most pairs are negative. Their total contribution can outweigh a ranking improvement on rare positives.

Judge B · Average precision

“Did you put the true examples near the top?”

AP follows precision and recall as we move down a class’s ranked predictions. Mean AP averages those class AP values, giving each included class equal weight.

For detection AP, boxes must also match ground truth at the required IoU. The current experiment uses IoU 0.5.

Now replace the analogy with measured evidence.

The direct numerical audit found zero language as the global BCE optimum in all three seeds. The development AP curves preferred substantially more language.

These are gate-fitting BCE and internal-development cached-crop AP, measured on different internal partitions. They are not final detection f-mAP. Points show the tested weights; connecting lines are visual guides. The weight axis expands small values using a log-like scale.

What we verified

Direct bounded optimization also selects zero global language weight. Removing class shrinkage does not reveal a large positive BCE optimum. Negative-pair loss accounts for most of the increase between the pure expert endpoints.

What this does not prove

It does not prove language is useless, that an encoder collapsed, or that calibration alone will fix the problem. Three seeds share one video split. Development ranking gains still need a detector-level check.

6

Zoom all the way in: one crop, one label.

Let i be a candidate crop and c a composition label. Each label has its own sigmoid score because the task is multilabel.

First, mix the two probabilities.

pic = (1 − g) p5ic + g pLic

g is the language weight. Stage 5 gets 1 − g. With g = 0.75, a Stage 5 score of 0.20 and a phrase score of 0.80 become 0.65.

Then, apply detector confidence once.

sic = qi × pic

For q = 0.80, that example becomes 0.80 × 0.65 = 0.52. This is the score used in detection ranking; it is not automatically a calibrated 52% chance of a correct detection.

The old gate learned g by minimizing BCE.

ℓ(p, y) = −y ln(p) − (1 − y) ln(1 − p)

When y = 1, the loss is −ln(p). When y = 0, it is −ln(1 − p). The gate averaged this over crop/class pairs. The experts themselves were trained with class-weighted focal loss, a different objective.

The new test selects g by development AP.

g* = arg maxg ∈ tested grid mean86 triplets APdevelopment(g)

There is no new gradient-based gate fitting in this test. We compare the finite grid, record the winner, and evaluate that fixed mixture. This is selection of a hyperparameter.

Why multilabel BCE makes sense, but does not settle the choice of objective

A crop can have multiple valid labels. Separate sigmoid predictions allow multiple labels to receive high scores; a single softmax over all labels would force them to compete for a sum of one. BCE is therefore a sensible multilabel baseline. However, its probability penalty and weighting across examples need not match macro AP or the desired tail behavior. Valid-composition constraints are a separate issue; multilabel BCE does not enforce them on its own.

Global, class-wise, and scene-dependent: three different seesaws

Global: one g for every crop and composition. This is the current AP-selected test.

Class-wise: gc changes with the class but not the crop. The previous study fit 135 logits, shrinking them toward a learned global logit.

Scene-dependent: gc(x) changes with the crop’s evidence. This could use expert scores or disagreement, but disagreement is not proof that language is correct. It has not been tested in this experiment.

The old class-gate penalty and optimizer audit
gc = sigmoid(ac)
Loss = mean BCE + (λ / 135) Σc (ac − a0)²

a0 was the fixed fitted global logit. Unsupported classes fell back to that global value. The audit directly solved unregularized BCE in weight space, including the exact endpoints, so it did not depend on a sigmoid starting near zero. The convex numerical audit clipped expert endpoints to [10⁻⁷, 1−10⁻⁷], a slight numerical difference from clipping the final mixture in training.
